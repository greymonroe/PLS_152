Course information

The primary aim of this course is to help you develop a solid foundation in basic genetics so that you will be able to apply this knowledge to understand new and emerging discoveries in the field and evaluate how these discoveries have shaped our understanding of plant evolution, ecology and can be used for crop improvement.

The course material ranges from classical (transmission) genetics to plant molecular and quantitative genetics, genomics, and epigenetics. Because we will cover a broad range of topics, this will limit the extent to which we can explore any particular field in depth.

One theme throughout the course is how plant biology and genetics are interconnected. Another theme is that the development of new molecular tools has significantly advanced our understanding of genetic mechanisms. They have provided new ways to speed up plant breeding, which is essential to ensuring global food security.

This course emphasizes critical thinking, problem-solving and the synthesis of information: grades are not determined by memorizing facts. The prerequisite for PLS 152 is BIS 2A, and the class is taught accordingly. Students who have mastered BIS 101 may find some of the initial lectures basic, but this changes as the quarter progresses, and the core concepts become more complex. There are many additional opportunities for advanced students to be challenged at every stage of the course.

We may have to reframe our goals depending on how much progress we make, but wherever possible, we will take your perspective into account.

PLS 152 is taught in person in Veihmeyer Hall 212, Tuesdays and Thursdays from 3:10 to 5:00 PM. Although the registrar lists a lecture plus a separate 20-minute discussion, we meet as one continuous block with a short break in the middle — plan for the full 3:10–5:00.

I hope you actively participate in this course because I have found it is the best way to engage you in learning. Slides are posted on this website before each class, but some of the best days of the quarter only happen in person: a greenhouse tour, guest panels, in-class activities, and discussions. If something prevents you from attending, contact me and we will work out how you can keep making progress.

Where things live: all course materials — schedule, slides, readings, assignment details — are on this website. Canvas is where you submit things (quizzes, reflections, assignments) and where every deadline is listed.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this course, students should be able to:

  • Explain various ways in which plant biology affects genetic diversity and inheritance (dominance, meiosis, recombination, mating systems, mutation) and how genetic variation affects plant biology (how variation in DNA sequence affects plant traits).
  • Interpret results from plant genetics research, including reading primary research articles.
  • Apply knowledge of plant genetics to real-world problems in agriculture.
  • Appreciate plant genetics memes.

Prerequisites and materials

Students should have completed BIS 2A or an equivalent course covering the fundamental concepts of molecular biology and genetics. Students looking to refresh their knowledge are encouraged to use freely available resources, including non-conventional ones such as YouTube and Wikipedia. If you come across a concept or term that you don’t know, sometimes a simple Google search can be really effective.

There is no required textbook. Assignments are submitted via Canvas. Each week pairs a primary research article with the lecture material (see readings) — quiz questions draw on them, and learning to read real papers is part of the point. Bring a laptop on Thursday October 15 (the library session and midterm work day).

Assignments and assessment

Class participation (10%): a short reflection on each class, submitted on Canvas and graded for completion, due by 11:59 PM the same day. Two questions, every time: what interested you most today, and what would you benefit from a deeper explanation of? I read these, and they shape the following lectures.

Weekly assignments (30%): low-stakes quizzes on Canvas covering the previous week’s material, due Tuesdays at 3:10 PM, before class. Each quiz includes at least one question drawn from that week’s research article. The lowest weekly assignment score is dropped automatically.

Midterm (30%): a written mini literature review on a plant genetics topic of your choice — maximum 1,500 words, at least 5 peer-reviewed sources, due Tuesday November 10 at 3:10 PM. The full prompt, including guidance on choosing a topic, is on the midterm page. The October 15 library session launches your literature search, and the Topic Explorer (due October 22) helps you pick your topic with evidence instead of guesswork. Midterms are graded anonymously against a rubric posted with the assignment.

Capstone project (30%): the final for this class is a group presentation (5–10 minutes) exploring a plant genetics topic — presentations run December 1 and 3. Groups form from a preference survey (due October 29), with two short check-ins along the way. Grading is based on a rubric covering the presentation’s content and quality, plus each member’s individual contribution statement. Full details, timeline, and advice on the capstone page — worth reading early, since finding a topic and meeting potential groupmates both start well before November.

Extra credit (up to 2%): memes! Post an original genetics meme to the class gallery by Friday December 11 — 1 point, or 2 if it’s plant genetics specific. A proud course tradition.

Grading policy

Your success in this class is important to me. We will all need accommodations because we all learn differently. If there are aspects of this course that prevent you from learning or exclude you, please let me know as soon as possible. Together we’ll develop strategies to meet both your needs and the requirements of the course.

“Time Banks” late assignment policy: students have a two-day grace period for one assignment or two one-day extensions for two different assignments. Additional late work is eligible for 60% of original points. Time Banks do not apply to the final project (a scheduled presentation) or to daily reflections — a reflection on today’s class only makes sense today; the completion grading and drop-lowest rules are the built-in slack. For extraordinary circumstances (e.g. illness, emergency), please contact me as soon as possible to make arrangements.

Use of AI

AI tools are part of how modern science gets done, and this course will teach you to use them: the October 15 library session covers AI research assistants for finding and understanding scientific literature. You are welcome to use AI to search for papers, get unstuck on a concept, test your understanding, or get feedback on your writing.

What you submit must be your own work. Do not turn in writing generated by AI. For the midterm in particular: every citation must be a real paper that you actually found and read. AI tools regularly invent convincing-looking citations, and a fabricated citation in your midterm is an academic integrity violation, not a technology accident. You are accountable for everything you submit, whatever tools helped along the way.

Academic integrity

All members of the UC Davis community benefit from an environment of trust, honesty, fairness, respect, and responsibility. You are expected to present your own work and acknowledge the work of others in order to preserve the integrity of scholarship. This includes submitting your own original work, proper citation of all sources of information, keeping quiz content to yourself, and not submitting work completed for another class without prior permission. Violations of the Academic Integrity policy can result in dismissal from the university and a permanent notation on a student’s transcript. For the full policy, see the Academic Misconduct page at the Division of Undergraduate Education.
